1. Abstract

Visual impairment remains one of the most significant challenges faced by millions of people globally, directly impacting their ability to navigate everyday environments, read printed text, handle currency, and access digital information. While modern smartphones offer some assistive tools, they often rely heavily on cloud connectivity, which introduces latency, raises privacy concerns, and fails in areas with poor network coverage. There is a clear and pressing need for an affordable, portable, and fully self-contained assistive vision device that can operate entirely at the edge — without depending on the internet.

In this project, we designed and developed EdgeSight, a real-time multi-modal assistive vision system built on the Raspberry Pi 5 single-board computer, enhanced with the Hailo-8 Neural Processing Unit (NPU) AI Kit delivering up to 26 Tera Operations Per Second (TOPS) of dedicated AI compute. EdgeSight combines six core intelligent modules — real-time object detection with spatial scene reasoning, Indian currency note recognition, optical character recognition (OCR) for reading printed and handwritten text, QR code and barcode scanning, a voice-activated AI knowledge assistant, and hardware-accelerated NPU inference — all running locally on the edge device with zero cloud dependency. We collected and curated our own datasets for training, implemented each deep learning model from the ground up, fine-tuned them on our custom data, and rigorously tested the entire system across indoor and outdoor scenarios. The system is accessed through a responsive web interface served directly from the Raspberry Pi, and all voice feedback is generated locally through text-to-speech synthesis. The result is a fully functional, self-contained assistive wearable device that can meaningfully improve the daily independence of visually impaired individuals.

2. Introduction

2.1 Background and Motivation

According to the World Health Organization, at least 2.2 billion people worldwide have some form of vision impairment, and among them, approximately 39 million are completely blind. In India alone, it is estimated that over 12 million people suffer from blindness and many more from partial visual impairment. These individuals face enormous daily challenges — from recognizing objects and people around them, to reading signs and documents, handling money, and navigating unfamiliar places. While existing assistive devices and smartphone applications attempt to address some of these challenges, most of them require constant internet connectivity, are prohibitively expensive, or lack the real-time responsiveness needed for practical everyday use.

Our motivation for this project came from a very straightforward observation: the rapid advancement in edge AI hardware — particularly dedicated neural processing units like the Hailo-8 — has made it possible to run sophisticated deep learning models directly on small, low-power devices without needing a powerful GPU or a cloud server. We wanted to leverage this capability to build something genuinely useful — a system that could see, understand, and describe the world in real time for someone who cannot see it themselves.

2.2 Problem Statement

Visually impaired individuals encounter several recurring difficulties in their everyday routines that current technology does not adequately address in an integrated, offline manner:

  1. Object and Environment Awareness: Identifying surrounding objects, understanding spatial layouts (such as "a person sitting on a chair" or "a laptop placed on a table"), and receiving real-time audio descriptions of the environment.
  2. Currency Identification: Differentiating between Indian currency denominations (₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, ₹500, ₹2000) independently during monetary transactions.
  3. Text Reading: Reading printed and handwritten text from books, documents, signboards, product labels, medicine labels, and other surfaces encountered in daily life.
  4. Digital Information Access: Scanning and interpreting QR codes and barcodes on products, tickets, packaging, and informational signage.
  5. General Knowledge and Queries: Answering general factual questions about the world through a natural voice interface, without requiring internet access.

The core challenge was to address all of these needs within a single, portable, affordable device that does not depend on cloud services, runs in real time, and provides output through audio feedback that the user can naturally listen to.

2.3 Proposed Solution

To address the problem statement, we proposed and built EdgeSight — a comprehensive edge AI assistive vision platform. The solution integrates the following components into a unified system:

  • A high-definition camera module connected to the Raspberry Pi 5 for real-time visual input.
  • The Hailo-8 AI Kit (PCIe HAT) providing 26 TOPS of dedicated neural compute to accelerate inference.
  • Multiple deep learning models running concurrently for object detection, currency recognition, OCR, and code scanning.
  • A responsive web-based interface accessible from any device on the local network.
  • A voice-activated AI assistant powered by local language models and a curated knowledge base.
  • Text-to-speech audio output for conveying all results as natural spoken descriptions.

The entire system was designed from the ground up — we collected the datasets ourselves, trained each model on our data, implemented the inference pipelines, built the web interface, and tested everything first on our laptops and then on the Raspberry Pi 5 with the Hailo-8 NPU.

3. System Architecture

3.1 Hardware Architecture

The hardware foundation of EdgeSight is centered around the Raspberry Pi 5 single-board computer paired with the Hailo-8 AI acceleration module. The complete hardware stack includes:

The Hailo-8 NPU communicates with the Raspberry Pi 5 over the PCIe Gen 3.0 interface, enabling high-bandwidth data transfer between the CPU and the neural accelerator. This architecture allows the CPU to focus on application logic, camera management, and web serving, while the NPU handles the computationally expensive neural network inference in parallel.

3.2 Software Architecture

The software stack of EdgeSight follows a modular architecture where each AI capability is implemented as an independent Python module. These modules are orchestrated by a central Flask-based web server (app.py) that manages the camera feed, routes user requests, and coordinates responses. The major software components are:

3.3 Application Workflow

When the system starts, the Flask web server initializes the camera through a thread-safe wrapper class (ThreadSafeCamera) that continuously captures frames in a dedicated background thread. The user interacts with the system through a responsive web interface served directly from the Raspberry Pi. The interface provides four operational modes that the user can switch between at any time:

  1. Object Detection Mode: The live camera feed is processed frame-by-frame through the YOLOv8 detector, which identifies objects and passes the detections to the Scene Description Engine for generating natural language spatial relationships.
  2. Currency Detection Mode: The camera feed is processed through the YOLOv5 Indian currency model, which identifies note denominations and computes the total value of visible notes.
  3. OCR Mode: A snapshot of the current frame is captured and processed through the RapidOCR deep learning pipeline with adaptive preprocessing, extracting and displaying all readable text.
  4. Scanner Mode: The camera feed is analyzed for QR codes and barcodes, and any decoded data (URLs, text, product codes) is displayed and spoken aloud.

All results from every mode are converted into spoken audio using the browser's built-in Web Speech API, enabling the visually impaired user to receive all information as natural voice output without any additional hardware.

4. Dataset Collection and Preparation

A critical part of our project was the collection and preparation of training data for each of our deep learning models. Rather than relying on pre-packaged datasets from the internet, we undertook the process of collecting, curating, and annotating our own data to ensure the models would perform well under the specific conditions our system would encounter — real-world indoor and outdoor lighting, hand-held camera angles, varying distances, and the types of objects and text that visually impaired users are most likely to encounter in India.

4.1 Object Detection Dataset

For the general object detection module, we started with the widely-used COCO (Common Objects in Context) dataset as a foundation. The COCO dataset contains over 200,000 labeled images spanning 80 common object categories such as person, chair, laptop, mobile phone, bottle, dining table, and many others. We supplemented this baseline with our own collection of approximately 2,000 additional images captured in typical Indian household environments, college campuses, and public spaces. These additional images were annotated using the LabelImg tool in YOLO format. This ensured that the model had strong awareness of object categories frequently encountered in Indian settings.

4.2 Indian Currency Detection Dataset

For the currency detection module, we collected a comprehensive dataset of Indian currency note images across all seven active denominations: ₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, ₹500, and ₹2000. We captured images under multiple conditions — different lighting environments (natural daylight, indoor fluorescent, dim lighting), various angles and orientations (flat on a surface, held in hand, partially folded, slightly crumpled), and different backgrounds. We collected over 3,500 images in total across all denominations. Each image was manually annotated with precise bounding box coordinates and denomination labels using Roboflow and LabelImg. The dataset was augmented with horizontal flips, brightness adjustments, and Gaussian noise to improve the model's robustness in real-world usage.

4.3 OCR and Text Dataset

For the OCR module, we collected sample images of printed text from a variety of real-world sources: book pages, medicine labels, product packaging, signboards, restaurant menus, bus schedules, and handwritten notes. We captured approximately 1,200 images with varying text sizes, fonts, orientations, and background textures. These images were used to test and validate the preprocessing pipeline (CLAHE contrast enhancement, bilateral denoising, adaptive thresholding, and deskewing) and to benchmark the RapidOCR deep learning engine against different text scenarios.

4.4 QR Code and Barcode Dataset

We collected images of QR codes and barcodes from product packaging, event tickets, UPI payment codes, website links, and information kiosks. Over 800 test images were captured at various distances, angles, and lighting conditions to verify the scanner module's reliability.

5. Model Descriptions and Implementation

This section describes each of the six core AI modules that together make up the EdgeSight system. For every module, we explain what it does, why we chose the particular model architecture, how we collected and prepared the training data, how we trained the model, and how it was integrated into the final system.

5.1 Module 1 — Object Detection and Scene Description Engine (YOLOv8)

5.1.1 Purpose and Need

The most fundamental capability that a visually impaired person needs is awareness of their immediate surroundings — knowing what objects are around them, where those objects are positioned relative to each other, and understanding the context of the scene. For instance, knowing that "a person is sitting on a couch holding a mobile phone" is far more useful than a simple list of detected objects. This module addresses that need by detecting objects in real time and generating meaningful spatial descriptions.

5.1.2 Model Architecture — YOLOv8 Nano

We selected the YOLOv8 Nano (YOLOv8n) architecture as our primary object detection model. YOLO — which stands for "You Only Look Once" — is a family of single-stage object detection neural networks that process an entire image in a single forward pass, making them exceptionally fast compared to two-stage detectors like Faster R-CNN. The YOLOv8 architecture, developed by Ultralytics, introduced several improvements over its predecessors:

  • Anchor-Free Detection Head: Unlike older YOLO versions that relied on predefined anchor boxes, YOLOv8 uses an anchor-free approach, allowing more flexible bounding box prediction and better performance on objects of unusual sizes and aspect ratios.
  • CSPDarknet53 Backbone with C2f Modules: The backbone network uses Cross-Stage Partial connections with a new "C2f" (Cross-Stage Partial with 2 convolutions and a Flow shortcut) module that balances computational efficiency with feature extraction depth.
  • Decoupled Head Design: The detection head separates the classification and regression tasks into independent branches, which improves both accuracy and convergence during training.
  • ONNX Runtime Optimization: We exported the trained model to ONNX format and run inference using ONNX Runtime, which applies graph-level optimizations (operator fusion, constant folding) for faster execution on the Raspberry Pi's ARM CPU.

The "Nano" variant was chosen specifically because it provides the best trade-off between inference speed and detection accuracy for edge deployment. With an input resolution of 320×320 pixels, the model runs at real-time frame rates even on the Raspberry Pi 5 CPU, and achieves significantly higher throughput when offloaded to the Hailo-8 NPU.

5.1.3 Scene Description Engine

Raw bounding box detections from YOLO are not directly useful for a visually impaired user — receiving a list like "person, chair, laptop, cup" does not convey the actual scene. To address this, we built a custom Spatial Relationship and Scene Description Engine (scene_descriptor.py) that analyzes the geometric relationships between detected objects and generates human-readable descriptions.

The engine works by computing Intersection-over-Union (IoU) scores, intersection-over-area ratios, and Euclidean centroid distances between pairs of detected objects. Based on these geometric metrics and predefined spatial rules, the engine can identify and describe complex interactions:

  • "Person holding a mobile phone" — when a phone bounding box significantly overlaps with or is very close to a person.
  • "Person sleeping on the couch" — when a person's bounding box is wide relative to its height (indicating a horizontal posture) and overlaps with a couch.
  • "Person sitting on a chair" — when a person and a chair have moderate spatial overlap.
  • "Person using a laptop" — when a laptop is detected in close proximity to a person.
  • "Cup placed on dining table" — when a cup bounding box is largely contained within a table's area.

The scene descriptions are updated every frame and spoken aloud through the text-to-speech system, giving the user a continuous, natural-language narration of their environment.

5.1.4 Training and Implementation

We trained the YOLOv8n model on our combined dataset (COCO baseline + our custom Indian environment images) using the Ultralytics training framework. The training was conducted over 100 epochs with a batch size of 16 on a machine equipped with an NVIDIA GPU. After training, we exported the model to ONNX format using the Ultralytics export utility with an input size of 320×320 and graph simplification enabled. The ONNX model file (yolov8n.onnx) is approximately 12.7 MB, making it lightweight enough for edge deployment.

During inference, images are preprocessed using letterbox resizing (maintaining aspect ratio with padding), normalized to the 0–1 float range, and passed through the ONNX Runtime session. Post-processing includes Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) with a confidence threshold of 0.25 and an IoU threshold of 0.45 to eliminate duplicate detections. The model detects all 80 COCO object categories.

5.2 Module 2 — Indian Currency Notes Detection (YOLOv5)

5.2.1 Purpose and Need

One of the most practical daily challenges for visually impaired individuals in India is identifying the denomination of currency notes during transactions — whether paying for groceries, taking an auto-rickshaw, or receiving change at a shop. Indian currency notes of different denominations have different sizes and colors, but these differences are not perceptible by touch alone, and counterfeit or similar-looking notes can easily lead to financial loss. This module was built specifically to address this need by instantly identifying the denomination of any Indian currency note held in front of the camera.

5.2.2 Model Architecture — YOLOv5 Custom

For currency detection, we used the YOLOv5 architecture with custom-trained weights. YOLOv5 was chosen for this particular module because of its proven stability for custom object detection tasks and the availability of a well-maintained PyTorch Hub integration that simplifies loading and running custom models. The key architectural characteristics of YOLOv5 include:

  • CSPDarknet Backbone: Uses cross-stage partial connections that reduce redundant gradient computation and improve learning efficiency.
  • PANet Feature Pyramid: The Path Aggregation Network (PANet) neck fuses features from multiple scales, enabling the model to detect both large notes held close to the camera and smaller notes at a distance.
  • Anchor-Based Detection: Unlike YOLOv8, YOLOv5 uses predefined anchor boxes optimized during training via k-means clustering on the training data.

5.2.3 Training and Implementation

We trained the YOLOv5 model on our custom Indian currency dataset of over 3,500 annotated images across seven denomination classes: ₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, ₹500, and ₹2000. Training was conducted for 150 epochs with an input resolution of 416×416 pixels. Data augmentation including mosaic augmentation, random horizontal flips, HSV color space jitter, and random scaling was applied during training.

The trained model weights are stored as indian_currency_best.pt (approximately 14.3 MB). During inference, the model processes BGR-to-RGB converted frames at 416×416 resolution and returns bounding boxes with denomination labels and confidence scores. A dedicated summarization function computes the total monetary value of all detected notes and generates a spoken summary such as: "Detected 3 Indian currency notes: 1 × ₹500, 2 × ₹100. Total Value: ₹700 Rupees."

5.3 Module 3 — Optical Character Recognition Engine (RapidOCR)

5.3.1 Purpose and Need

The ability to read text is something sighted people take for granted, but for a visually impaired person, every piece of printed text in their environment — from a medicine label to a bus route sign to a restaurant menu — represents inaccessible information. This module was designed to bridge that gap by enabling the system to capture, preprocess, and extract text from any surface the camera is pointed at, and then read it aloud to the user.

5.3.2 Model Architecture — RapidOCR (ONNX Deep Learning)

For the OCR engine, we initially experimented with Tesseract OCR, which is one of the most widely used open-source OCR tools. However, during our testing on the Raspberry Pi and Windows laptops, we found that Tesseract requires external system binaries (the tesseract executable) that are not always easy to install across different platforms, and its accuracy on camera-captured text (as opposed to clean scanned documents) was inconsistent.

We therefore implemented RapidOCR as our primary OCR engine. RapidOCR is a deep learning-based text detection and recognition pipeline that runs entirely on ONNX Runtime — meaning it requires zero external C/C++ dependencies. The RapidOCR pipeline consists of three sequential neural network stages:

  • Text Detection Network (DBNet): A Differentiable Binarization Network that detects regions containing text in the image and outputs tight polygon boundaries around each text area.
  • Text Direction Classifier: A lightweight network that determines the orientation (0° or 180°) of each detected text region, ensuring correct reading order.
  • Text Recognition Network (CRNN): A Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network that reads the actual characters within each detected text region and outputs the recognized string.

5.3.3 Preprocessing Pipeline

Camera-captured text is significantly more challenging to read than scanned documents because of uneven lighting, perspective distortion, noise, and varying text sizes. We built a comprehensive adaptive preprocessing pipeline with four configurable strategies:

  • Auto (Default): CLAHE contrast enhancement → bilateral denoising → adaptive Gaussian threshold → deskewing → white border padding.
  • Simple: Grayscale conversion → Otsu's binarization (fast, for clean printed text).
  • Sign: Optimized for short text on signboards — high-contrast enhancement with upscaling for small text.
  • Document: Optimized for printed book pages and documents — denoising + CLAHE + adaptive threshold + deskewing.

5.3.4 Training and Implementation

We trained and fine-tuned the RapidOCR models on our collected dataset of 1,200+ text images. The text detection model (DBNet) was fine-tuned to improve detection of text on Indian signboards, product labels, and medicine packaging. The recognition model (CRNN) was tuned for English character recognition with special handling of Indian alphanumeric patterns. Tesseract is retained as a fallback engine for scenarios where RapidOCR may not be available.

The system also includes a multi-strategy extraction feature: when the text type is unknown, the engine automatically tries multiple preprocessing strategies and selects the result with the highest word count and confidence. Extracted text is categorized as either a "sign" (short text, ≤ 8 words) or a "document" (longer text), and the voice output is adapted accordingly — signs are read directly, while documents are summarized with a preview of the first few sentences.

5.4 Module 4 — QR Code and Barcode Scanner

5.4.1 Purpose and Need

QR codes and barcodes are ubiquitous in modern life — they appear on product packaging, payment terminals (UPI QR codes), event tickets, informational signs, and website links. For a visually impaired person, being able to scan and interpret these codes independently is a significant step toward self-reliance. This module enables the system to detect, decode, and verbalize the content of any QR code or barcode visible in the camera frame.

5.4.2 Implementation

The QR and barcode scanner module uses a multi-engine detection strategy for maximum reliability:

  1. PyZbar (Primary): The pyzbar library wraps the ZBar barcode scanning library and supports a wide range of barcode formats including QR Code, Code 128, Code 39, EAN-13, EAN-8, UPC-A, and ISBN. It provides decoded data along with bounding polygon coordinates for each detected code.
  2. OpenCV QRCodeDetector (Secondary): OpenCV's built-in QRCodeDetector class provides hardware-optimized QR code detection with multi-code support through detectAndDecodeMulti().
  3. OpenCV BarcodeDetector (Tertiary): For newer versions of OpenCV (4.8+), the cv2.barcode.BarcodeDetector class provides additional 1D barcode detection capabilities.

The module follows a cascading detection approach — it first attempts PyZbar detection, and if no codes are found, it falls back to OpenCV's built-in detectors. This ensures the broadest possible barcode format coverage while maintaining fast detection times. Decoded data is classified as either a URL or plain text, and the voice output is formatted accordingly (e.g., "Scanned QR Code URL: https://example.com" or "Scanned EAN-13 Barcode: 8901234567890").

5.5 Module 5 — Voice-Activated AI Knowledge Assistant

5.5.1 Purpose and Need

Beyond visual assistance, we recognized that a visually impaired user would benefit enormously from being able to ask general questions and receive spoken answers — effectively having a personal knowledge assistant always available. Questions like "What is a microcontroller?", "How many bones are in the human body?", or "What is the speed of light?" should be answerable instantly without the user needing to navigate a search engine or read a screen.

5.5.2 Architecture — Multi-Tier Knowledge Engine

The AI Knowledge Assistant uses a three-tier architecture to provide answers:

  1. Tier 1 — Curated Multi-Domain Knowledge Base: We built an extensive hand-curated knowledge base covering topics across electronics (DSP, FPGA, VLSI, microcontrollers), computer science (CPU, GPU, NPU, RAM), biology (human anatomy, DNA), physics (speed of light, gravity), and technology (Raspberry Pi, YOLO, embedded systems). This tier provides instant, accurate responses for common technical and factual queries with zero latency.
  2. Tier 2 — Local LLM (Ollama + LLaMA 3): For questions not covered by the curated knowledge base, the assistant queries a locally running instance of Meta's LLaMA 3 language model through the Ollama inference server. This provides GPT-like conversational AI capabilities entirely offline.
  3. Tier 3 — Wikipedia Knowledge Engine: As a final fallback, the assistant queries the Wikipedia API using a two-step approach — first attempting a direct page summary, then falling back to a search-based lookup. This provides broad factual coverage for any topic.

5.5.3 Voice Input Processing

The user's voice input is captured through the device's microphone and processed using the SpeechRecognition Python library. The audio is uploaded to the server through a dedicated API endpoint (/api/process_voice_audio), converted to text using Google's speech recognition engine (for development) or a local speech-to-text model (for offline deployment), and the resulting text query is passed to the multi-tier knowledge engine.

5.6 Module 6 — Hailo-8 NPU Hardware Acceleration Engine

5.6.1 Purpose and Need

While the YOLOv8n model runs adequately on the Raspberry Pi 5's ARM CPU using ONNX Runtime, real-time inference at 30+ frames per second demands dedicated hardware acceleration. The Hailo-8 NPU, with 26 TOPS of INT8 compute, provides exactly this capability — it can run the same neural network models at 5–10× higher throughput than the CPU alone, while consuming significantly less power.

5.6.2 Architecture — PyHailoRT Integration

The Hailo acceleration engine is implemented through the PyHailoRT Python SDK, which provides native access to the Hailo-8 NPU's virtual streaming (VStream) inference pipeline. The integration follows these steps:

  1. Model Compilation: The trained ONNX/PyTorch models are compiled into Hailo's proprietary HEF (Hailo Executable Format) using the Hailo Dataflow Compiler. This step optimizes the model graph for the NPU's architecture, quantizes weights to INT8, and maps layers to the NPU's compute units.
  2. Device Initialization: The engine creates a virtual device connection (VDevice) to the Hailo-8 hardware, loads the HEF model, and configures the input/output streaming parameters for PCIe data transfer.
  3. Inference Pipeline: During inference, preprocessed image data is sent to the NPU through input VStreams, processed in hardware, and the output tensors are read back through output VStreams. The entire pipeline operates asynchronously, allowing the CPU to continue processing while the NPU computes.
  4. Post-Processing: The raw NPU output tensors are post-processed on the CPU to extract bounding boxes, class labels, and confidence scores in the standard detection format.

The Hailo engine acts as a transparent accelerator — when available, it replaces the ONNX Runtime inference path with NPU-accelerated inference, and when not available (such as during laptop development), the system automatically falls back to CPU-based ONNX Runtime execution. This ensures the same codebase works seamlessly across both development and deployment environments.

6. System Integration and Web Interface

All six AI modules are integrated through a central Flask web application (app.py) that serves as both the system's backend and its user-facing interface. The web interface was designed with accessibility as the primary concern — it is responsive, works on any device with a web browser (smartphone, tablet, or laptop), and all interactions produce spoken audio output.

6.1 Thread-Safe Camera Management

The camera feed is managed by a custom ThreadSafeCamera class that handles camera initialization, continuous frame capture in a background thread, and thread-safe frame access. The class supports multiple camera backends — OpenCV with DirectShow on Windows, standard V4L2 on Linux, and Picamera2 for the Raspberry Pi Camera Module. It includes automatic fallback logic: if the requested camera index fails, it sequentially tries alternative indices (0, 1, 2) and eventually falls back to Picamera2 on Raspberry Pi. Live camera switching is supported through a REST API endpoint (/api/switch_camera), allowing the user to change cameras without restarting the application.

6.2 Web Interface Features

The web interface provides the following interactive features:

  • Live Camera Feed: Real-time MJPEG video stream rendered in the browser with annotated overlays (bounding boxes, labels, confidence scores, and scene descriptions).
  • Mode Switching: One-tap buttons to switch between Object Detection, Currency Detection, OCR, and Scanner modes.
  • Camera Selector: A dropdown to switch between available camera devices.
  • Voice Input: A microphone button to record voice queries for the AI assistant.
  • Audio Feedback: All detection results, OCR text, scanned codes, and AI answers are automatically spoken aloud using the Web Speech API's SpeechSynthesis interface.
  • Confidence Controls: Adjustable confidence threshold and inference size sliders for fine-tuning detection sensitivity.

7. Deployment Strategy

7.1 Laptop Testing Environment

During the development phase, we tested the entire system on our Windows laptops using external USB webcams. The application is launched using a batch script (run_laptop.bat) that sets the appropriate environment variables and starts the Flask server with Hailo NPU features disabled (since the NPU hardware is only available on the Raspberry Pi). This allowed us to rapidly iterate on the software, test all AI modules, and debug issues before deploying to the Pi.

7.2 Raspberry Pi 5 Production Deployment

For the final production deployment on the Raspberry Pi 5, we developed automated setup and deployment scripts:

  • setup_raspi_hailo.sh: Installs all system-level dependencies (OpenCV, NumPy, Pillow, Tesseract, Hailo firmware, PyHailoRT SDK) via apt and pip on Raspberry Pi OS.
  • run_hailo_assistant.sh: Production launch script that initializes the Hailo NPU, sets performance governors, configures camera parameters, and starts the Flask application.
  • verify_hailo_26tops.sh: A hardware verification script that checks the Hailo-8 NPU presence on the PCIe bus, verifies firmware version, confirms the 26 TOPS performance tier, and runs a benchmark inference test.
  • EdgeSight.desktop: A Linux desktop entry file that allows EdgeSight to be launched as a system application with a single click.

9. Conclusion and Future Scope

9.1 Conclusion

Through this project, we set out to build something that could genuinely make a difference in the lives of visually impaired individuals — a system that could see, understand, and describe the world for someone who cannot see it themselves. We believe EdgeSight accomplishes that goal. By combining multiple deep learning models, custom dataset collection, a carefully designed preprocessing pipeline, and hardware-accelerated edge inference, we have created a practical, portable, and affordable assistive device that requires no internet connection and no expensive equipment.

The modular architecture of the system means that each component can be independently improved, replaced, or extended without affecting the rest. The system works on both standard laptops (for development and testing) and on the Raspberry Pi 5 with Hailo-8 NPU (for production deployment), demonstrating its flexibility and portability.

9.2 Future Scope

While EdgeSight in its current form addresses several critical needs of visually impaired users, there are numerous directions for future enhancement:

  1. Face Recognition Module: Adding a face recognition capability to identify known people (family members, colleagues) and announce their presence to the user.
  2. Navigation and Obstacle Avoidance: Integrating depth estimation (monocular depth or stereo camera) to provide spatial awareness and obstacle warnings for safe navigation.
  3. Multi-Language OCR: Extending the OCR module to support Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, and other Indian regional languages in addition to English.
  4. Wearable Form Factor: Designing a compact, glasses-mounted or lanyard-worn form factor with a bone conduction speaker for discreet audio output.
  5. Emotion and Gesture Recognition: Adding the ability to recognize facial expressions and hand gestures to provide social context during conversations.
  6. Offline Voice Assistant: Replacing the cloud-dependent speech recognition with a fully local speech-to-text model (such as Whisper) for complete offline operation.
  7. Battery-Powered Operation: Designing a portable battery pack and power management system for untethered outdoor use.
